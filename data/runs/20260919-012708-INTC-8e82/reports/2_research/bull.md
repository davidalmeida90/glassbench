## 1. Growth Potential: The Trough Is In, and the Math Gets Violent From Here

The single most important number in that entire fundamental report is this: **Q1 FY26 revenue grew +7.2% YoY to $13,577M** — after revenue had been flat at ~$53B for three straight years (2023–2025). That is the first clean sign that the demand cycle has bottomed. When a company stops shrinking and starts growing again, the operating-leverage math flips fast.

Now layer on the margin trajectory:

| Period | Gross Margin |
|---|---|
| FY2024 | 32.7% |
| FY2025 | 34.8% |
| **Q1 FY2026** | **39.4%** |

That's a **670 bps expansion in gross margin off the FY24 trough** — and 250 bps of it came in a single year-over-year quarter. This is not a stalling business; this is a business whose core unit economics are *repairing in real time*. The bear will point at the -$3.1B operating loss in Q1 FY26 — but I'll get to why that's a below-the-line story in a moment. What matters for the growth thesis is that **gross profit rose 14.4% YoY** while revenue rose 7.2%. That's positive operating leverage at the gross level — the engine is turning over.

And then there's the capex lever, which is the real fireworks:

| FY | Capex |
|---|---|
| 2023 | $25.75B |
| 2024 | $23.94B |
| 2025 | **$14.65B** |
| Q1 FY26 (annualized) | **~$14.5B** |

Capex is **down ~43% from the 2023 peak**, and Q1 FY26 capex fell nearly 30% YoY. Here's the punchline: operating cash flow is still running at **~$10B TTM**. When you combine a fall in capex from $25.7B to ~$14.5B with stable-to-growing revenue and a rising gross margin, **you are staring directly at a free-cash-flow inflection.** The bear wants to value INTC on trailing FCF of -$3.1B. I'll value it on where FCF goes when capex normalizes against a growing, higher-margin revenue base — and that's a fundamentally different company than the one the trailing screens show.

## 2. Competitive Advantages: You Can't Build a Second One of These

This is the part of the bear case that consistently gets underweighted. Intel is one of a **handful of companies on Earth with leading-edge semiconductor manufacturing capability**, and it is the *only* Western-scale IDM alternative to TSMC for advanced nodes. That is not a marketing claim — it's a strategic fact with enormous pricing power implications.

Consider what that buys Intel:
- **The IDM model as a moat.** Intel designs AND fabricates. Its 18A/14A roadmap isn't just a product story — it's an *external foundry* story. If even a handful of anchor customers adopt Intel's leading-edge nodes, Intel converts from a declining PC/server vendor into a Western sovereign foundry with multi-year contracted revenue. That is a category change, not an incremental improvement.
- **Strategic scarcity.** In a world where semiconductor supply chains are explicitly a national-security priority, the only US-headquartered leading-edge logic manufacturer has a value that trailing multiples cannot capture. That scarcity value is precisely why the market is willing to pay 10x sales for a currently-unprofitable company.
- **The capital-intensive barrier to entry.** The reason Intel had to spend $25B/yr on capex is the same reason nobody can easily replace it: fabs cost billions and take years. That's a moat in a downturn and a cash machine in an upturn.

The bears love to say "it's losing money, so it's not competitive." I'd counter: **it's *investing* money at a scale and technical frontier that only a few firms globally can match — and it just started pulling that investment back as the demand cycle turns.** That's the definition of a company positioning for the up-cycle.

## 4. Preemptive Rebuttals to the Bear Case I Know Is Coming

**Bear argument #1: "10.2x sales and 90x EV/EBITDA with negative earnings — it's insanely expensive."**

This is the classic trailing-multiple trap. You cannot value a turnaround on trailing EBITDA when EBITDA is cyclically depressed by a demand trough, a gross-margin reset, and peak capex. The market isn't paying 90x *current* EBITDA — it's paying for the EBITDA that exists when gross margin holds at 39%+, capex stays at ~$14.5B, and revenue grows. Run that math and the multiple compresses dramatically without the stock moving a dollar. The bear is quoting the price of the past and calling it the price of the future.

**Bear argument #2: "Q1 FY26 operating loss of -$3.1B shows the turnaround is failing."**

Read the report carefully: **gross profit ROSE 14.4% YoY while operating income fell.** That divergence tells you the loss is coming from *below the gross line* — R&D ramp, restructuring, impairments — not from the core business. Intel is knowingly spending to build 18A/14A and restructure. Those are discretionary, front-loaded investments, not structural cash leaks in the product business. The core — revenue and gross margin — is *improving*. That's the signal; the below-the-line noise is the distraction.

**Bear argument #3: "FY2024's -$18.8B loss and volatile earnings prove the business is broken."**

FY2024's loss was dominated by **impairments and restructuring** — non-cash, one-time, and exactly the kind of action you *want* a turnaround to take early. FY2025 was essentially **breakeven (-$267M)**. And note the report's own observation: Q3 FY25 swung to +$4,063M on non-operating items. So the clean operating trend is *improvement* masked by the accounting swings. The bear is reading volatility as weakness; I'm reading it as a company mid-surgery, with the patient's vitals (revenue, gross margin, OCF) trending the right way.

**Bear argument #4: "It just dropped 16% in four days — the top is in."**

A 16% pullback after a 143%-above-the-200-SMA, parabolic, RSI-86 advance is not a top — **it's a pressure release.** The report's own framework says the trend remains intact and the key battleground is the 107.78 VWMA / 111.65 10 EMA. Price is holding the VWMA right now. RSI has reset from 86 to 62 without breaking 50. MACD is at 15.00 with a histogram of just 0.11 — meaning any bounce immediately re-steepens momentum. This is precisely the setup where dip-buyers re-engage, not where the thesis dies. And critically — the report notes the selling is **"not yet climactic"** and volume on down days (135M) is *below* the 227.7M up-day volume. That's not distribution; that's a pause.
